In July 1938, representatives from thirty-two countries gathered in Évian-les-Bains, France, to address the growing crisis of Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi Germany and Austria. The Anschluss had taken place four months earlier. Anti-Jewish legislation had intensified. Property seizures, professional bans, and imprisonment were accelerating.

The conference produced sympathy and no action. Most participating nations declined to expand immigration quotas. The United States maintained the national origins system established under the Immigration Act of 1924. Britain limited entry to Mandatory Palestine. Canada offered no meaningful increase in admissions. The Dominican Republic was the lone country to signal willingness to accept larger numbers.

Évian confirmed that borders wouldn’t open wider.

By May 1939, when Britain issued the White Paper restricting Jewish immigration to Palestine to 75,000 over five years, escape routes were narrowing through policy, not force. Within weeks, ships would begin testing those limits.

On May 13, 1939, the German liner MS St. Louis departed Hamburg with 937 passengers, most of them Jewish refugees holding Cuban landing certificates. Germany had already stripped Jews of citizenship under the Nuremberg Laws. Kristallnacht had destroyed synagogues and sent thousands to concentration camps. Emigration had become urgent and bureaucratically complex.

The ship arrived in Havana on May 27. During the voyage, Cuba’s government under President Federico Laredo Brú had invalidated most landing permits amid domestic political pressure. Only 28 passengers were allowed to disembark. The remaining 900-plus were denied entry.

Jewish relief organizations negotiated from Havana Harbor. Cuba formally refused admission. Captain Gustav Schröder sailed north along the Florida coast. The ship came within sight of Miami. Appeals went to the Roosevelt administration. The United States didn’t authorize emergency visas. Canada declined as well.

On June 6, 1939, the St. Louis turned back toward Europe.

Schröder secured agreements from Belgium, the Netherlands, France, and Great Britain. The ship docked in Antwerp on June 17. Approximately 288 refugees were admitted to Britain. The remaining 619 were distributed among Belgium, France, and the Netherlands.

After Germany invaded Western Europe in 1940, many of those passengers again fell under Nazi control. Research by the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents that 254 of them were later murdered. The majority survived, often through hiding, resistance networks, or secondary flight. The voyage did not end in Havana Harbor. It ended across occupied Europe.

The United States admitted roughly 200,000 Jewish refugees between 1933 and 1945, primarily through existing quota allocations. The German and Austrian quotas were often unfilled, but visa processing was slow and heavily scrutinized. Assistant Secretary of State Breckinridge Long instructed consular officials to apply strict documentation requirements, a directive that effectively reduced the number of visas issued. Financial affidavits, background checks, and administrative delays narrowed entry further.

Ships carrying passengers with valid visas did dock in New York, Baltimore, and New Orleans. After 1940, Lisbon became the principal departure point for those who had secured documentation in time. The SS Quanza, which left Lisbon in 1940, carried refugees initially refused entry in Mexico. After negotiations involving Jewish advocacy groups and American officials, eighty-one passengers were permitted to disembark in Norfolk, Virginia.

Galveston, Texas, had served as an alternative immigration port earlier in the century through the Galveston Movement, which redirected Jewish immigrants away from New York between 1907 and 1914. By the late 1930s, most refugee arrivals came through East Coast ports.

Admission depended on paperwork completed before departure. Without visas in hand, ships found few options once at sea.

Britain’s 1939 White Paper limited Jewish immigration to Palestine and restricted land purchases, a response to Arab opposition during the 1936–1939 Arab Revolt. After the quota of 75,000 was reached, further immigration would require Arab consent.

Despite those restrictions, clandestine maritime efforts known as Aliyah Bet attempted to transport refugees to Palestine.

In November 1940, the Patria carried approximately 1,800 Jewish refugees whom British authorities intended to deport to Mauritius. Haganah members planted explosives to disable the ship and prevent deportation. The explosion exceeded what was intended. The vessel sank in Haifa harbor, killing more than 260 people.

In December 1941, the Struma, carrying nearly 800 Romanian Jews, reached Istanbul after engine failure. British authorities denied entry to Palestine. Turkey refused disembarkation. After weeks anchored in harbor, Turkish officials towed the disabled ship into the Black Sea. On February 24, 1942, a Soviet submarine torpedoed it. One passenger survived.

The Mediterranean became a corridor defined by restriction, interception, and maritime risk.

In December 1940, the Salvador, a small Bulgarian vessel carrying refugees toward Palestine, sank in the Sea of Marmara. More than 200 people drowned.

The Struma remains one of the deadliest refugee maritime disasters of the war. These sinkings weren’t naval battles. They were the result of overcrowded vessels, mechanical failure, and political deadlock.

In July 1947, the Exodus 1947 departed France carrying more than 4,500 Holocaust survivors attempting to reach Palestine. The British Navy intercepted the vessel near Haifa. Passengers were forcibly transferred to other ships and returned to displaced persons camps in Germany.

Images of Holocaust survivors sent back to Germany intensified debate over British policy in Palestine. Within a year, Britain terminated the Mandate.

Between 1933 and 1945, approximately 300,000 German and Austrian Jews emigrated. Hundreds of thousands sought visas but couldn’t secure them in time. Canada admitted fewer than 5,000 Jewish refugees during the Nazi era. The United States admitted more, within a quota system that limited access and slowed processing. Britain restricted entry to Palestine through the critical prewar and wartime years.

Ships became instruments of policy. Passenger manifests, consular cables, and harbor logs document decisions made at desks as much as at sea.

The MS St. Louis remains the most widely remembered of these voyages because it approached the Florida coastline and was refused. It was neither the first nor the last vessel to encounter closed ports.

Maritime escape during the Nazi era depended on documentation secured before departure, diplomatic intervention, and the political will of receiving states. Where that will was absent, ships circled harbors, returned to Europe, or drifted into open water.

The sea did not decide their fate. Governments did.
