In the days after Graffiti passed, people reached out. Not just here or on Instagram. Everywhere. Emails, DMs, comments, messages from people I had never spoken to before. And what surprised me was not that they came. What surprised me was what they were actually saying.

They were not only writing to say they were sorry about Graffiti. Many of them had been following his journey for months, writing to me throughout his diagnosis, sharing their own experiences with a sick dog or navigating the same disease. But after he passed, something shifted. They were telling me about their own dog. The one they lost three years ago. The one they lost last month. The one they lost a decade ago and had never really talked about, not like this, not to anyone who they felt would understand. Story after story, and almost all of them carried the same thing underneath:

I loved them more than I knew how to explain, and when they were gone, no one around me fully got it.

I know that feeling. I have lived it, in a way I have not talked about publicly until now.

A few of you in our community recently asked if I would write about this. About why dogs are never just dogs, and why so many people carry that grief quietly, feeling like no one fully understands it. When I read those messages, something shifted in me, because they were describing something I have carried for a long time. And I am writing this from inside the grief, not from the other side of it.

Some of you who have been following on Instagram for a long time may remember Lani.

Lani was my first corgi. My first dog. I got her right out of college, and she was everything a first dog is when you are young and building your life and suddenly there is this creature at the center of all of it. She barked and attacked me playfully every single time I sneezed, without fail, like it was a personal offense she needed to address immediately. It made me laugh every time. She was with me for nine months. That was it. Nine months, and then she was gone.

I remember the morning after. I went to work because I did not know what else to do. I told my boss what had happened, and a few of the people I worked with. The response was not cruel, and it was not cold. It was just... insufficient. There was a pause, a kind look, maybe an I’m sorry, and then the day continued. The way it does when someone around you has lost something that does not register on the same scale as the grief you are actually carrying. I could feel it in the air, that unspoken sense of it was just a dog, and I should be functional by now, and this was not the kind of loss that warranted falling apart.

Nobody said those words to me directly. They did not have to.

You know when your grief is too big for the room you are standing in. You can feel it. And what happens next is you start to wonder if maybe you are the one who has it wrong. Maybe it should not hurt this much. Maybe everyone else has the proportions right and you are the one who lost perspective.

That was about twelve years ago. I carried it for a long time, not because I chose silence, but because there was nowhere to put it that felt safe.

Some of you reading this right now may be from that original group, and I want you to know that what you did back then is one of the most profound things that has ever happened to me. It was the moment I realized that people I had never met could care that deeply, and that a community built around the love of a dog could hold you when the people in your physical life could not. That small group of strangers carried me through one of the hardest things I had ever been through. I do not know how I would have processed it without them.

I think about that constantly right now. Because after Graffiti passed, it happened again. Bigger this time, and from so many more of you, but the same thing at its core. People showing up because they understood. And it is helping me in ways I am still trying to find words for.

But here is what I keep sitting with: not everyone has this. So many of the people writing to me are processing their grief for the first time, not because their loss is new, but because following Graffiti’s journey gave them a place to finally feel it. They are mourning alongside our dog to understand their own loss. And that tells me something important about what is missing.

Things have changed since then. More people see their dogs as family now than they did twelve years ago.

But not all the way.

I still hear it. Not always out loud, but in the silences, in the awkward pauses, in the well-meaning suggestions to get another one, as if the love you built with one specific being is interchangeable with the next. I hear it in the fact that most workplaces will not give you a single day off when your dog dies. I hear it in the way people expect you to return to normal within days, because the world has not yet decided that this kind of loss deserves the same weight as any other. I hear it in the stories so many of you have been sending me.
